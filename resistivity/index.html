Electrical Resistivity Surveys in Christchurch: Mapping Subsurface Conditions with Confidence

Sound ground. Sound decisions.

LEARN MORE

Christchurch sits on a complex braided river fan where the Waimakariri River has laid down gravels, sands, and silts over millennia, and the 2010–2011 Canterbury earthquake sequence permanently altered the shallow groundwater table across much of the city. A conventional borehole tells you what happens at one point, but a resistivity profile reveals how these water-bearing layers connect between drill locations — and that becomes critical when you are siting infiltration basins in Riccarton or tracing saline intrusion near the Avon-Heathcote Estuary. The method measures how easily the ground conducts an injected electrical current, and because wet clays and silts conduct far better than dry gravels or clean sands, the resulting cross-sections give us a continuous image of stratigraphy and pore-fluid chemistry. For sites where liquefaction assessment has flagged loose saturated sands at depth, resistivity can help map the lateral extent of those units without a single extra borehole. When the target is deeper than about thirty metres — say, for a production bore in the deep gravel aquifers that supply Christchurch’s drinking water — we switch from a fixed-spread array to Vertical Electrical Sounding, progressively expanding the electrode spacing to sample progressively greater depths in a controlled sequence.

A resistivity cross-section often shows the continuity of the aquitard that a grid of boreholes can only guess at — and in Christchurch’s layered gravels, that continuity controls both settlement and seepage.

Approach and scope

The contrast between Christchurch’s western suburbs and the eastern plains shows up starkly in resistivity data. Out toward Burnside and Bishopdale, the dry, gravel-dominated Riccarton Gravel Formation produces high-resistivity signatures, often above 200 ohm-m, which makes it relatively straightforward to pick the top of the water table and to differentiate clean gravels from interbedded silt lenses. Move east into the Avonside–Bexley corridor, and the picture changes: post-quake groundwater is often within half a metre of the surface, and the interleaved estuarine silts and peats drop bulk resistivity below 20 ohm-m, sometimes as low as 3 ohm-m where saline pore water is present. This low-resistivity environment demands careful array design — we typically use a Wenner array for vertical resolution in these conductive soils and process the data through a solid inversion routine that accounts for the known surface-water layer. The result is a depth-calibrated resistivity section that clearly separates the swamp deposits from the underlying fluvial gravels, which is exactly the information a geotechnical engineer needs to plan dewatering or to design stone columns as a ground-improvement solution without hitting unexpected soft layers at depth. We also run dipole-dipole surveys along proposed trench alignments in the Port Hills, where loess-colluvium contacts can hide shear zones that a resistivity line will flag as a sharp lateral conductivity contrast.

Site-specific factors

Christchurch’s post-earthquake rebuild triggered a wave of foundation innovation, but it also left behind a patchwork of remediated and unremediated land where buried organic silts and variable groundwater can confound a standard site investigation. We have seen projects on the eastern side where three boreholes spaced twenty metres apart encountered three completely different groundwater chemistries because of old stream channels filled with estuary mud — and a resistivity line run between those boreholes explained the puzzle in an afternoon. Skipping this step when you are designing deep excavations or stormwater soakage on the Canterbury Plains risks missing a perched saline layer that will corrode steel piles or a paleochannel that will daylight into your excavation. The Canterbury Regional Council’s stormwater disposal rules effectively require you to demonstrate the absence of a shallow confining layer, and resistivity tomography provides that evidence with fewer invasive points. For sites within the Christchurch Central Recovery Plan area, resistivity data also supports the seismic microzonation framework by constraining the depth to the base of the soft soil profile, which feeds directly into site-class determination under NZS 1170.5.

Q&A

How much does an electrical resistivity survey cost in Christchurch?

Most Christchurch projects fall between NZ$1.120 and NZ$1.930 for a day of fieldwork plus processing. A short 2D line with 48 electrodes on a flat urban section sits at the lower end; a deeper VES sounding or a long line requiring traffic management and topographic correction will move toward the upper end. Every quote includes the inversion report, depth-calibrated sections, and a briefing call with your geotechnical engineer.

Can resistivity distinguish between salt water and clay in the eastern suburbs?

Both saline pore water and saturated estuarine clay produce very low resistivity, often below 5 ohm-m, so they can look similar on a raw cross-section. We separate them by integrating the resistivity line with at least one calibration point — usually a CPT soil behaviour type log or a water-sample conductivity measurement. The resistivity inversion then constrains the clay–salt boundary, which is essential for predicting steel-pile corrosion rates.

What surface conditions limit resistivity testing in Christchurch?

Asphalt and dry gravel ballast create a high contact resistance that can degrade data quality. On sealed carparks we drill small pilot holes through the asphalt to plant electrodes in the underlying soil, and on railway ballast we use longer electrodes with bentonite slurry to improve coupling. Extreme dryness in summer can also reduce signal penetration in the top metre, so we schedule surveys for early morning or after light rain when the near-surface moisture is more favourable.

How deep can a resistivity survey see in the Canterbury gravels?

With a Wenner array and a 10 m electrode spacing, we typically achieve good resolution to about 12–15 m depth. A VES survey with a Schlumberger expander reaching 100 m can sound to 40–50 m in the Riccarton Gravel, which is sufficient to reach the top of the deep confined aquifer system. The actual depth of investigation depends on the resistivity contrast between layers; in Christchurch’s gravel-over-silt sequences the contrast is strong, so the depth estimate is usually reliable.
